CASE STUDY 01 · FISERV SHIPPED TO PRODUCTION

Agents now work a dispute without leaving one workspace

I redesigned Fiserv's Disputes Workspace so its navigation follows the life of a dispute, and agents no longer move between tools to finish one case.

THE PROBLEM Agents with 30 to 50 open cases and regulatory deadlines moved between separate tools for intake, review and chargebacks. The navigation followed the systems, not the dispute.
WHAT I DID I rebuilt the navigation around the five stages of a dispute and put authorization matching next to the decision, so one workspace covers the whole case.
HOW WE GOT THERE I had no direct access to agents. I walked SMEs and product managers through the legacy app and my designs, and checked every assumption against requirements, QA and release feedback.
THE OUTCOME Four tools became one. Task steps fell from 6 to 4, screens per case from 8 to 5, and navigation from 4 levels to 2. Desjardins and Target launched with a 100% UAT pass rate and zero UX-related defects.

◇ 01 · From tools to lifecycle

From system boundaries to the dispute lifecycle

Agents handle about 30 to 50 active cases at a time, each on a clock set by FCBA and Reg E. In the legacy setup they moved between separate tools for intake, authorization review, fraud and non-fraud work, and chargebacks. The navigation followed those system boundaries, so an agent had to know which tool held the next step.

BEFORE · BY SYSTEM
  1. Intake in one tool
  2. Authorization review in another
  3. Fraud and non-fraud work in their own tools
  4. Chargeback work in its own tool
AFTER · BY LIFECYCLE
  1. Intake
  2. Transaction and authorization review
  3. Validation
  4. Pre-dispute or chargeback processing
  5. Resolution and monitoring

The top level became Cases, Work Queues, Reports and Admin. DWS did not retire every upstream system. ACSM, Document Manager and other products still do their own jobs, and DWS became the place the agent works from.

◇ 02 · Where the evidence came from

No direct access to agents, so I checked everything twice

I could not observe agents directly, so this was not primary research with them. The evidence came from product managers, business analysts, engineers, QA, internal SMEs and client requirements.

I interviewed product managers, operations leaders and compliance partners. I walked SMEs through the designs, and I mapped the current state from documentation, workflow diagrams and operational reports. Product managers also took me through the legacy app, so I knew which fields were optional, required or editable before I finalized a workflow.

I treated what stakeholders told me as assumptions. Then I checked them against SME walkthroughs, requirements reviews, QA feedback, release evidence and post-release feedback.

◇ 03 · What I tried before it worked

What I tried before it worked

1.

Confidence as a percentage

An early Disputes GPT pilot concept showed "85% confident". Stakeholders pointed out that a number did not tell an agent what to do. I replaced it with High, Medium and Low labels, each paired with guidance to accept, verify key fields, or review manually.

2.

Fixing screens after translation

Desjardins needed Canadian French, which runs longer than English. I dropped the idea of patching screens one by one after translation. Every UI string was externalized and the layouts were built to hold their shape as text grew.

3.

One middle-ground workflow for every role

Call-center agents, fraud analysts, dispute processors and compliance officers do different jobs. A single compromise would have served none of them well. What shipped is role-relevant navigation on one shared design language.

What limited the design, and what we did

◇ 05 · Where AI helps, and where it stops

Where AI helps, and where it stops

Disputes GPT is a pilot. It reads a dispute described in plain language and pre-fills 6 to 10 intake fields, each marked High, Medium or Low. The agent confirms, edits or overrides every value, and the audit log keeps both the suggestion and the final value. It does not approve or deny disputes. The full design is on its own page.

The Virtual Analyst, which clears repetitive cases, is separate and has no AI in it. It runs only the actions and queries that people write and assign.

The component system behind these screens, including the move to Fiserv's Pixel design language and the 45+ components built to WCAG 2.1 AA, is covered on the design system page.
